The Library of Alexandria, founded in the early third century BCE during the reign of Ptolemy II Philadelphus, was one of the largest and most significant libraries of the ancient world. Located in the Egyptian city of Alexandria, the library was part of a larger research institution called the Mouseion, which was dedicated to the Muses.

At its height, the library is estimated to have housed between 400,000 and 700,000 scrolls, covering subjects ranging from mathematics and astronomy to medicine, philosophy, and literature. Scholars from across the Mediterranean world were invited to study there, and the library became a hub of intellectual activity for several centuries.

The exact circumstances of the library's destruction remain a matter of historical debate. Various accounts attribute its decline to fires during Julius Caesar's siege in 48 BCE, later imperial decrees, and gradual neglect over the following centuries. By the seventh century CE, the library had effectively ceased to exist, taking with it an immeasurable portion of ancient knowledge.
